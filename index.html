Unbalanced Oil and Vinegar

UOV is a digital signature scheme that remains secure even against quantum computers. It is built from the trapdoored multivariate quadratic maps by following the hash-and-sign paradigm. First proposed by Aviad Kipnis, Jacques Patarin and Louis Goubin in 1999 [KPG99], it could be seen as the unbalanced version of the original OV scheme proposed by Jacques Patarin.

UOV has been submitted to NIST Post-Quantum Cryptography Project, in response to NIST's Call for Additional Digital Signature Schemes for the PQC Standardization Process. In May 2026, UOV was selected as one of the the Round-3 candidate of the Additional Digital Signature Signatures for the Post-Quantum Cryptography Standardization Process.

Design

UOV is a hash-and-sign signature scheme whose public key is a trapdoored homogeneous multivariate quadratic map

\[ \mathcal{P} = (p_1,\ldots,p_m) : \mathbb{F}_q^n \to \mathbb{F}_q^m. \]

Verification evaluates \(\mathcal{P}\). Signing finds a preimage under \(\mathcal{P}\) using the trapdoor. Without the trapdoor, this is assumed to be computationally hard for appropriately chosen parameters.

History

Pat97 proposed the balanced Oil and Vinegar scheme, with \(n = 2m\). KS98 broke that case. KPG99 answered with Unbalanced Oil and Vinegar: \(n > 2m\).

Trapdoor

Public key. The map \(\mathcal{P}\) above. Evaluation \(\mathbf{x} \mapsto \mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\) is public and efficient. Without the trapdoor, finding a preimage of a random target is the UOV problem (Definition 2 in the specification).

Trapdoor. An \(m\)-dimensional subspace \(O \subseteq \mathbb{F}_q^n\) — the oil space — on which \(\mathcal{P}\) vanishes: \(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{o}) = \mathbf{0}\) for every \(\mathbf{o} \in O\).

Inversion. Since \(\mathcal{P}\) vanishes on \(O\), one has, for \(\mathbf{o} \in O\) and any \(\mathbf{x}\),

\[ \mathcal{P}(\mathbf{o}+\mathbf{x}) = Q(\mathbf{o},\mathbf{x}) + \mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}), \]

where \(Q\) is bilinear. To invert \(\mathcal{P}\) at a target \(\mathbf{t}\), sample a random vector \(\mathbf{x}\) and solve the linear system \(Q(\mathbf{o},\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{t} - \mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})\) for \(\mathbf{o} \in O\); then \(\mathbf{o}+\mathbf{x}\) is a preimage.

Without knowledge of \(O\), no efficient method is known for constructing this linear system. Recovering \(O\) from \(\mathcal{P}\) is called key recovery and enables efficient inversion of \(\mathcal{P}\). See security for the key-recovery attacks.

UOV scheme in brief

Key pair. Public \(\mathcal{P}\); trapdoor \(O\).

Sign message \(\mu\). \(\mathbf{t} = \mathrm{Hash}(\mu)\); find \(\mathbf{s} \in \mathcal{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{t})\) using \(O\); output \(\mathbf{s}\).

Verify. Accept iff \(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{s}) = \mathrm{Hash}(\mu)\).

Security

There is no formal proof reducing a well-known hard problem, such as MQ, to the security of UOV. Concrete analysis lists the known critical attacks that affect the hardness estimate.

Attacks fall into two types: key recovery, which recovers the trapdoor from the public key, and forgery, which forges a message-signature pair that passes verification.

Forgery

AttackIdea
Collision (§4.1) Find a match between values of \(\mathcal{P}(s)\) and \(\mathrm{Hash}(\mu \Vert \mathrm{salt})\) (OW96; SB24); use Gray-code enumeration to evaluate \(\mathcal{P}\) (BCC+10).
Direct (§4.2) Treat \(\mathcal{P}\) as a random underdetermined MQ instance and recover a preimage of \(\mathbf{t}=\mathrm{Hash}(\mu \Vert \mathrm{salt})\), via hybrid Wiedemann-XL, Thomae–Wolf, or Hashimoto (YCBC07; TW12; Has21).

Key recovery

AttackIdea
Kipnis–Shamir (§4.3) Generalize the polynomial-time \(n=2m\) case (KS98) to \(n>2m\) (CHDY11).
Intersection (§4.4) Find \(k\) linearly independent oil-space vectors by solving a quadratic system derived from intersections of transformed oil spaces (Beu21), using the reconciliation attack (DYC+08).
MinRank (§4.5) Find a minimal-rank linear combination of the public matrices (GC00; BG06; BBC+20; TPD21; Beu22; Peb25; SFI+25). For the four recommended parameter sets, the estimated MinRank attack costs are substantially higher than those of the other attacks considered.
Wedge (§4.6) First proposed for characteristic 2 via the exterior algebra (Ran26); later extended to any characteristic (JPH+26), and by combining the intersection attack with \(p^\ell\)-truncated rings (FI26). Hardness estimates rely on heuristic conjectures that do not always hold (Ran26; DLT26).

MEM-2D (§4.7). The cost estimates for the direct, intersection, and wedge attacks are based on the cost of their PWXL stages. The usual cost counts only that solver's gates, treating memory as free; \(\delta_{2D}\) is the extra memory-access cost of the same solver, reported only as a sensitivity analysis (DGY26).

Quantum (§4.8). Known quantum attacks apply Grover’s algorithm to exhaustive-search components of classical attacks; none is known to give more than a quadratic speedup.
